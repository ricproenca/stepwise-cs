## Ethics

- 3 example definitions (book uses 3rd):

- Ethics are the moral principles by which any person is guided

### Ethics

### Moral Principles

### Moral Principles

- Laws should reflect what is ‘right’ and ‘wrong’

### Pragmatism

- Pragmatism defines what is common sense – e.g. don’t murder etc

### Pragmatism

### Non-Adherence

### Non-Adherence

- You’re implementing software to control medical equipment – your friend is the owner of the company and takes a quick look, instantly deciding it’s ready to use
- Software hasn’t been thoroughly tested – not in interest of public, product, profession or even client

### Non-Adherence

- You are working on a team. They want to use Python or Java – you don’t know either language and insist on using assembly

### Non-Adherence

- Judgement – not independent. Also responsibilities to the public and product aren’t met

### Non-Adherence

- People signup for your newsletter. You send them each 100 spam emails per day, hoping that there may be some product they are interested in
- Profession – damages the integrity of the profession

### Non-Adherence

- A colleague submits some code that contains a bug. You laugh at them, call them an idiot and slander them to your boss
- Colleagues – not acting fair or reasonable to your colleagues

### Non-Adherence

- Conspiracy theories are popular – realizing this is an untapped market, you decide to create a load of websites spreading mis-information, but fill them with ads

## Ownership and copyright

### Copyright

- Copyright is a formal recognition of ownership
- If an individual creates and publishes work with an element of originality, they can claim copyright
- Exception is made if individual is working for an organization – organization can claim copyright for themselves/the individual

### Copyright

- Can’t be claimed on an idea
- Can’t be claimed on any part of published work that was previously published by different individual or organisation

### Media which can be copyright

- Literary (written) work
- Sheet music or musical recording
- Film
- Radio or TV broadcast
- Art
- Computer program

### Moral justification for copyright

- Creation can take time, effort, expertise and money
- Creation also requires original thinking
- Hence copyright holder should be able to earn money from their work, without others quickly copying it
- It’s also unfair that people who copy original can make money, without any payment to original creator

### Maps

- For 100s of years, many map companies often include fake places/roads/buildings/mountains etc on their maps – why do you think this is?
- Since if they see a map from another company with this same fake place, then they know they have copied them and can hence take legal action

### Reverse Engineering

- Software can be reverse engineered, though even easier, apparently, is pharmaceuticals
- Imagine a company has spent $100,000,000s trying to develop a cure for cancer – they might develop successful medicine and hence will recoup their investment
- The problem is, once created, another company – which has spent 0 on R&D, can determine the chemical composition of the medicine very easily, hence can start mass-producing it
- Hence, you can argue, that without strong copyright laws, companies may not invest in research and development, since after that huge spend, in some industries, others may be able to copy them for a fraction of the cost

### Copyright Laws

- Different countries have slightly different laws, but they mostly agree to a common set of rules
- This prevents – e.g. – someone copying someone’s idea and publishing it in another country which doesn’t have/have as strict copyright laws

### Typical Copyright Laws Include

- A requirements for registration recording the date of creation of the work
- A defined period when copyright will apply (e.g. the author’s life plus an additional 70 years)
- A policy to be applied if a copyright holder dies
- An agreed method for indicating copyright – e.g. the © symbol

### Usage of Copyrighted Material

- When copyright is in place, there will be implications for how the work can be used – this will be in the form of a statement – for example:
- “This Code may be published without permission as long as it is not changed in any way and it carries the copyright notice. Copyright © 1999 by the Association for Computing Machinery, Inc. and the Institute for Electrical and Electronics Engineers, Inc.”

### Usage of Copyrighted Material

- If someone has bought a copyrighted product, there are no restriction on copies being made, provided they are only for individual use – you could happily copy the textbook 1000 times if you wanted, provided you didn’t share it with others

## Software licensing

### Software licensing

- There are 2 licensing categories the book will discuss:
- Commercial licensing
- Open or free licensing

### Software licensing

- In many cases – software – like any other commercial product – is created at a cost, which the company obviously has to offset by, at minimum, at least breaking even

### Software licensing

- There is a legal difference between buying hardware and software:
- If you buy a computer, you are legally the owner of it
- If you buy software, the ownership remains with the developer – you simply have a license to use it

### Software licensing

- There are different ways software could be commercially-licensed:
- A fee is paid for each individual copy of the software
- Group licenses – e.g. package A might allow the software to be installed on 100 devices, package B on 500 devices etc
- Special rates may be available for educational use

### Shareware vs Freeware

- Shareware: full version of the software, but only for a limited period – e.g. will stop working after 2 weeks, for example
- Freeware: version that’s free forever, but with less features than the full version

### Shareware vs Freeware

- Can you think of an example of shareware and freeware?
- Shareware: logic.ly, anything with a trial period
- Freeware: Adobe Reader, WinRar – anything with additional, premium features

### Shareware vs Freeware

- So don’t get confused by the term ‘freeware’ – a version of the program is free forever, but it’s not open source or free software – that is both free forever AND the full version

### Commercial Software: Source Code

- For commercial software – whether shareware or freeware – the source code will never be made available to the user. Why is that?
- Since not only does it make no sense to reveal source code to competitors, but users themselves would be able to compile/edit the source code themselves (e.g. simply deleting the code that asks for a license)

### Commercial Software

- Likely to be long-term, stable and have required features the company desires
- Software may have been created to be used in conjunction with already installed software - e.g. Photoshop integrates well with Premiere Pro & After Effects
- Continuous support, detailed documentation and maintenance may be provided
- Users with license may be able to have their suggestions implemented
- Might adhere to regulations more than free alternatives (particularly if used for financial/medical/sensitive data etc)

### Open Source & Free Licensing

- For open licensing, there are again two major options: open source software and free software
- Both are global, non-profit organisations

### Open Source

- Source code is available to public, free of charge
- Attitude is that people will contribute their own changes/features
- User is free to use, modify, copy or distribute it in accordance to terms of license
- Millions of open source projects out there – Linux & Apache are probably the two biggest, powering the majority of websites in the world

### Free Software

- The ‘free’ more refers to the freedom a user has – they are free to use the software in any way they want
- There may be a small fee to cover distributing/hosting costs
- Is also open source
- ‘Copyleft’ – the condition that a user can modify and distribute their version, though they have to use the SAME license/conditions as the original

### Open Source & Free Software Advantages

- Full functionality might be available for free/nominal cost
- If full functionality not provided, user can download and change source code to meet their requirements
- Might be more secure - since code can be viewed by anyone, hence more bugs might be reported and fixed
- Collaborative efforts can produce large, complex projects
- Future developments and maintainence provided by users of software, rather than commercial company (who may stop providing support if it’s not financially-viable)

## Artificial Intelligence

### Artificial Intelligence (AI)

- Draws from many other disciplines including philosophy, psychology, neuroscience, mathematics, linguistics and control engineering

### Artificial Intelligence (AI)

- Generalised definition:
- “AI concerns the use of a computer or computer-controlled device to perform tasks normally associated with intelligent behaviour by humans.”

### Artificial Intelligence (AI)

- We will look at 5 aspects of human intelligence and how AI can be applied in these spheres:
- Problem solving
- Linguistics
- Perception
- Reasoning
- Learning

### Problem Solving

- There are 2 ways AIs can attempt to solve problems:
- Brute force (giving the illusion of ‘intelligence’)
- Expert systems (supervised learning)

### Naive Problem Solving Approach

- Take chess – a computer can simulate through millions of potential move sequences, calculating a score for the quality of those sequences – naturally, it will simply choose the best one
- In reality, this isn’t really intelligence – it’s simply taking advantage of the massive processing speed computers have for these kinds of tasks, compared to humans

### Expert Systems/Supervised Learning

- In this approach, the AI model is supplied with data and/or rules about the scenario – it can then use this vast amount of data to predict future events
- If, however, there is a situation the AI has never seen before, it will have no way of dealing with it – unlike a human, who could attempt to think of a new approach – e.g. an AI trained for chess couldn’t play Go

### Linguistics

- Currently, voice recognition and voice synthesis techniques are already developed and are increasingly successful – YouTube’s audio/speech-recognition system is a good example, which can accurately detect words, songs etc
- Another example is a customer support line/bot – an automated voice could ask you questions and it will understand your answers, giving you appropriate follow-up questions, trying to solve your queries

### Perception

- Traditionally, robots have been used to perform repetitive manufacturing tasks – if something unexpected happens that damages the robot, it will try to continue operating as normal, potentially damaging it further
- What is attempted to be developed are autonomous robots – those that can detect if they are damaged, in a similar way to how a human could detect if they had an injury

### Perception

- Another example of where perception is important is for driverless cars – a perception of both itself and its surroundings
- Imagine a car trying to park itself in a vacant space – what would it have to be aware of?
- What a valid car parking space is, the position of the other cars, its own size and relative position, how different commands will change its position etc

### Reasoning

- Imagine a situation – a robot soldier is on the battlefield. There is a grenade with 5 enemy soldiers and 1 friendly soldier in the blast radius – should the robot throw the grenade away?
- Or take a medical robot – it could enter a burning building to save 2 people, but then would be damaged and wouldn’t be able to treat the 50 people outside with serious/life-threatening injuries – what should it do?
- AI will have to have both logical & moral reasoning

### Learning

- Machine learning is a very active field – this definition is a machine improving its performance for a particular task as it gains experience – for example, detecting objects in a photo, detecting the type of cancer, speech-to-text conversion etc
- Usually, ‘experience’ comes in the form of massive data sets – using appropriate statistical algorithms, the AI system can improve its future predictions

### Learning

- Another example of machine learning is when a system can predict what a user wants (e.g. to buy), based on previous behavior
- For example, if previous data suggests that people who buy Harry Potter & the Philosopher’s Stone will also buy Harry Potter & the Chamber of Secrets, then it will recommend this 2nd book, for people who buy the first

### Learning

- Some machine learning algorithms classify data – e.g.
- an email being spam or not spam
- a lump being cancerous or not
- the demographic/interest group someone belongs to – e.g. “young”, “male”, “likes sport”, “dislikes books” etc

### Impact of AI

- These days, the majority of the world population has Internet access – companies with AI systems can therefore collect massive amounts of data
- As a result, some people have privacy concerns and worry about the data being used for malicious intents/accessed by criminals

### Impact of AI

- Other common concerns include:
- Safety (from self-driving cars to killer robots)
- Loss of jobs (robots replacing us)
- Increased wealth inequality (low-skilled jobs might be largely replaced)
- Environmental issues – materials required for electronics have to be sourced and disposed of
- Only a limited supply of resources used to create these electornics
